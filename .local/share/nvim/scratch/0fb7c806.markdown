The presence of hadiths of varying authenticity is a central reason for this and it is precisely this barrier that the project is meant to address in the long run: making classical texts accessible to those who have not studied the full breadth of hadith sciences. I understand your concern regarding the sensitivity of classical tafsir material and the importance of avoiding presenting the corpus as something intended for general readers or as an authority on hadith authenticity.

I think we may be viewing the purpose of the project from slightly different angles. From my perspective, the contribution of this work is primarily methodological and computational.

The corpus is intended mainly for NLP tasks and research purposes, not for laymen to read directly. I see it as a language resource that can support Arabic NLP and Islamic NLP research, including information extraction, semantic segmentation, retrieval systems, corpus linguistics, and evaluation/alignment studies for Arabic LLMs.

At the same time, I do believe the corpus itself is an important research contribution and should eventually be published as a research resource after sufficient validation and review. The goal would not be to present it as a religious authority, but rather as a computationally annotated scholarly corpus for research use.

The main novelty is the structured XML annotation pipeline for classical Arabic tafsir using LLMs, including semantic segmentation and tagging of elements such as Quran verses, hadith matn, isnad chains, sources, narrators, rhetorical analysis, fiqh-related content, and other scholarly structures.

I also see the human verification stage as an essential component of the project. The XML tags themselves can be evaluated and refined through annotation guidelines and expert review. This is exactly why I proposed the annotation interface in my previous email.

However, the LK segmenter mainly detects isnad chains appearing at the beginning of the hadith.
